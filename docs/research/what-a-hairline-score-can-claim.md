# What a hairline score and a clinic introduction can claim in the United States

**Research date:** 22 September 2026  
**Scope:** U.S. federal primary sources only (FDA.gov, FTC.gov, eCFR, Federal Register / statute text, agency guidance). Not legal advice. Not a classification of Seenmark.

**Product facts assumed for framing (not researched as facts):** adult member photographs own hairline; app places them in early / mid / late bands with a short menu; mid points at medicines worth asking a prescriber about (app does not prescribe); late explains a clinic conversation and can store an introduction request (v1 does not deliver it); member and clinic do not pay in v1; a later ticket may add a disclosed affiliate link next to a band.

## 1. When a consumer hairline photo score is a medical device (or software as a device) versus a general-wellness product

### Rule in plain language

Software is a medical **device** when it is intended for use in diagnosing disease or other conditions, or in the cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or intended to affect the structure or function of the body—unless a statutory exclusion applies. “Intended use” is objective: labeling, advertising, design, and how the product is offered and used, not the maker’s private label of convenience.

Congress carved out software that is **for maintaining or encouraging a healthy lifestyle and is unrelated to** diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention, or treatment of a disease or condition. That carve-out is statutory, not optional marketing language.

FDA’s current **General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices** (reissued 6 January 2026) is the main policy that draws the compliance line for low-risk wellness products. Two factors must both be true:

1. **Only** a general-wellness intended use, and  
2. **Low risk** to users (non-invasive, not implanted, no intervention/technology that needs device controls for safety).

FDA splits wellness intended uses into:

- **Category 1 — no disease/condition references:** claims about a general state of health or healthy activity (examples include weight management, fitness, relaxation, mental acuity, **self-esteem** including cosmetic-function claims related only to self-esteem, sleep, sexual function).  
- **Category 2 — disease/condition mentioned only in a lifestyle-risk / living-well frame:** promoting or tracking healthy choices that **may help reduce the risk of** or **may help living well with** certain chronic diseases/conditions—**only** where that lifestyle role is generally accepted in peer-reviewed literature or professional-organization statements. Framing is “may help,” not diagnose/treat.

Products **fall outside** general wellness when labeling, advertising, UI, or functionality includes, among other things: references to **specific diseases, clinical conditions, or diagnostic thresholds**; alerts/prompts that **recommend or require specific clinical action or medical management**; **treatment guidance** intended to inform or direct medical management; claims of clinical equivalence/accuracy/“medical grade” or substitution for an FDA-authorized device; or intended-use statements aimed at diagnosis, screening, monitoring, or management of a disease or condition.

A soft “see a professional” notice can still fit wellness if it does **not** name a disease/condition, does **not** call outputs abnormal/pathological/diagnostic, does **not** include clinical thresholds, diagnoses, or **treatment recommendations**, and does **not** act as ongoing disease-management alerts.

Separately, FDA’s **Policy for Device Software Functions and Mobile Medical Applications** (September 2022) says software that **performs patient-specific analysis and provides patient-specific diagnosis or treatment recommendations to patients** (or other non-HCP users) is in the **focus of FDA oversight** as a device. By contrast, camera apps that only **document or transmit** photos (FDA’s example: skin lesions or wounds) for a consult with a professional are listed under **enforcement discretion**. Logging/tracking for general fitness/health/wellness without disease diagnosis/treatment use can be outside the device definition or low-risk under related policies.

**Statutory Non-Device CDS** (clinical decision support) under FD&C Act § 520(o)(1)(E) is built for software that supports recommendations **to a health care professional** who can independently review the basis—and it does **not** apply if the function is intended to acquire, process, or analyze a **medical image** (or certain device/signal sources). Patient-facing diagnosis/treatment recommendations are not what that carve-out is for.

**Hair / dermatology examples from FDA (closest available):**

- General Wellness: reminder software to keep skin out of sunlight that **may help reduce the risk of skin cancer** (Category 2 lifestyle framing).  
- General Wellness / risk: laser for “rejuvenating the skin” / appearance confidence is **not** low risk.  
- MMA policy: phone-camera apps for **documenting/transmitting** skin-lesion or wound photos to clinicians → enforcement discretion; **patient-specific analysis** that yields diagnosis/treatment recommendations to patients → oversight focus.  
- FDA’s published General Wellness examples do **not** give a hairline-score or alopecia-staging product. No FDA primary source located in this pass that classifies “early / mid / late hair thinning bands” by name.

## 2. Whether labeling the score “not medical advice” changes that line

### Rule in plain language

**No.** FDA determines intended use from the **whole** of claims, design, and circumstances of distribution—not from a contradictory disclaimer. If the product’s bands, menus, and marketing still diagnose a condition or recommend medical management, a “not medical advice / for entertainment only” line does not pull it back into wellness or out of the device definition.

FTC applies the same idea to **consumer net impression**: a disclaimer that **contradicts** express treatment claims is ineffective. FTC’s Health Products Compliance Guidance Example 14 is a smartphone acne app that claims treatment and then says it is “for entertainment purposes only and is not intended for the treatment of any disease or medical condition”—FTC says that disclaimer does **not** negate the treatment claim.

## 3. What the FTC requires for health-related claims, testimonials, and before-and-after photos (including hair loss)

### Rule in plain language

Under the **FTC Act**, unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce are unlawful. The Commission’s **Policy Statement on Deception** and **Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation** require that ads not mislead reasonable consumers about material facts, and that advertisers have a **reasonable basis** for objective claims **before** they run.

For **health-related** claims, FTC’s *Health Products Compliance Guidance* requires **competent and reliable scientific evidence**—generally well-conducted human clinical evidence (often RCTs for efficacy/disease claims). Claims are judged by **net impression** (words + images + name), including implied claims. Disclosures must actually change what consumers take away; fine-print or vague qualifiers often fail.

**Testimonials / endorsements (16 CFR Part 255):**

- An endorsement may not convey any claim that would be deceptive if the advertiser said it directly.  
- Consumer testimonials about a key attribute are usually read as **typical** of what users generally achieve; if that is not true, the ad must **clearly and conspicuously disclose the generally expected performance**, with substantiation for that disclosure. “Results not typical” / “you should not expect similar results” is **unlikely** to cure atypical testimonials.  
- **Baldness / hair-growth example (explicit in the Guides):** a page of hair-growth testimonials requires competent and reliable scientific evidence that the product produces new hair growth; atypical-result disclaimers do not fix a typicality claim without typical-result substantiation.  
- Before-and-after style imagery can itself communicate efficacy or disease-treatment claims (Health Products examples with before/after photos). Stock or mismatched photos that overstate improvement are deceptive (Endorsement Guides examples for acne / weight-loss imagery).

**Hair-loss-specific FTC.gov primary material:**

- Endorsement Guides baldness-treatment testimonial example (above).  
- FTC press release / settlement: *“Hair Farmer” Settles FTC Charges* (12 Nov 1998)—false/unsubstantiated baldness-cure claims; order barred selling products claiming to treat or prevent baldness and barred misrepresenting testimonials as typical. That is enforcement history, not a separate “hair statute.”

There is **no** separate FTC hair-loss advertising code located on FTC.gov beyond the general health-claim and endorsement rules plus case-specific orders.

## 4. Paid recommendations / affiliate links (endorsement guides): disclosure, and whether a “score” that routes to a paid product is a special problem

### Rule in plain language

**Material connections** between an endorser and a seller that might affect the weight of the endorsement—and that a significant minority of the audience would not reasonably expect—must be disclosed **clearly and conspicuously** (16 CFR § 255.5). Affiliate commissions count. Disclosure must communicate the **nature** of the connection well enough for consumers to evaluate it; it need not recite every contract detail.

FTC staff FAQ elaborates (agency guidance, not a separate statute):

- Say something like: “I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post.”  
- Place the disclosure **close** to the recommendation/link; if review and link are separated, one distant disclosure may fail.  
- “Affiliate link” or “commissionable link” alone is **not** clear enough; “**Paid link**” next to the link can be adequate for the link’s nature.  
- Disclose in both video/UI content **and** near links when both are used.

**Special problem when a ranking / score drives the paid path:**

Under 16 CFR § 255.4 Example 3 (headphone **review / ranking** site):

- Accepting money **in exchange for higher rankings** is deceptive **even with** a disclosure that the operator receives manufacturer payments—because payment **determines** the ranking.  
- If the site does **not** sell rankings but receives affiliate (or similar) payments from some makers, it must **clearly and conspicuously disclose** those payments.  
- A ranking **methodology that systematically favors** products whose sellers have a relationship with the operator **because of** those relationships is also misleading.

So: a disclosed affiliate link next to an independently generated band can be structured to fit the Guides; a **score or ranking that is bought, or that is methodologically tilted by who pays**, is the special problem—disclosure does not fix paid placement dressed up as an objective score.

A site that **falsely appears independent** while promoting its own commercial interest is also deceptive (§ 255.4 Example 2).

## 5. Clinic introductions: promising results, “verified” clinics, guaranteed outcomes

### Rule in plain language

Neither FDA nor FTC publishes a hair-clinic-introduction rule set. The same general advertising law applies to **services** and introductions:

- **Do not promise results you cannot substantiate.** Objective claims about outcomes, typical results, or superiority need a reasonable basis before advertising (substantiation policy; health guidance when health outcomes are claimed). Testimonials about clinic results carry typicality rules (§ 255.2). Celebrity/clinic paid promotion examples in § 255.5 require disclosure **and** typicality substantiation when consumers would take the experience as typical.  
- **“Verified,” seals, or independent-sounding badges:** if consumers would think an independent check occurred, the check must match what is claimed. A review site that **looks independent** but is not is deceptive (§ 255.4 Example 2). Paid seals from a bona fide third-party certifier that consumers **reasonably expect** to charge a fee may not require fee disclosure (§ 255.5 Example 10)—but that does **not** authorize selling a “verified” badge without the stated verification. Misrepresenting the basis of a hospital/expert “selection” is deceptive when the real basis is irrelevant or undisclosed (§ 255.3 Example 4 patterns in Part 255).  
- **Guarantees / “guaranteed results”:** 16 CFR Part 239 (*Guides for the Advertising of Warranties and Guarantees*) addresses advertising of warranties and satisfaction/money-back style guarantees (full refund if you say “satisfaction guaranteed,” disclose material conditions). The Deception Policy Statement notes that a money-back guarantee does **not** cure otherwise deceptive advertising. No FDA.gov or FTC.gov primary source located in this pass that adopts ABHRS’s “guaranteed results are inappropriate” language for hair restoration specifically; the federal constraint is the general ban on deceptive/unsubstantiated outcome claims plus warranty/guarantee advertising guides—not a hair-specific guarantee ban.

**FDA angle on introductions:** storing a request to be introduced to a clinic, without analyzing medical images for diagnosis or issuing treatment directives, is closer to a **communication / logistics** function than to device software focused in the MMA policy—but if the **score** that triggers the introduction is itself a patient-specific diagnosis or treatment recommendation, the score—not the inbox—is the device risk. FDA does not, in the sources above, specially regulate “clinic lead introductions.”

## Bottom lines for Seenmark drafting

### The line a consumer score should stay on if it wants to remain general wellness

- Frame the product as **tracking appearance / hairline photos over time** for awareness or self-esteem-style wellness, **without** naming or staging a disease/condition, and **without** diagnostic thresholds.  
- Menus, if any, stay at **general lifestyle / educational** material or a soft, non-disease-named suggestion that talking with a professional may be useful—**not** patient-specific medicine plans, dosing, or “you are mid-stage alopecia, start drug X.”  
- Photo handling that **stores and shows the member’s own photos** (and optionally lets them share with a clinician they choose) stays nearer the MMA “document/transmit” lane than analysis-for-diagnosis.  
- Keep risk low: phone camera only; no clinical-grade claims; no substitution for an FDA-cleared device.  
- Align **all** labeling, app store text, UI, and marketing with that narrow intended use (General Wellness + 21 CFR 801.4).

### The line it crosses into device / deceptive-claim territory

- **Device / SaMD risk:** patient-specific analysis of the member’s photos (or other inputs) that outputs a **diagnosis, severity stage, screening decision, or treatment recommendation** to the member—especially menus that **guide medical management** (prescription medicines as what this band needs; clinic as medically indicated next step). References to specific clinical conditions or diagnostic banding in UI/ads. Analyzing member photos **as medical images** for diagnosis also sits poorly with the Non-Device CDS carve-out’s medical-image exclusion. A “not medical advice” badge does not undo those intended uses.  
- **Deceptive-claim risk (FTC):** unsubstantiated hair-growth, restoration, or typical-result claims; before/after or testimonial imagery that implies typical outcomes without typical-result proof; “verified” or independent-sounding clinic badges that overstate the check; **paid rankings** or a score methodology that favors who pays; promising or guaranteeing surgical/clinic outcomes without a lawful, substantiated basis (and without treating “guarantee” advertising rules as a free pass for false efficacy).

### What a paid menu link must disclose

- Clear, conspicuous, unavoidable disclosure of the **money connection** (e.g., commissions on purchases through the link), in plain language, **next to** the recommendation/link.  
- Do **not** rely on “affiliate link” alone.  
- Do **not** let payment change the **band/score/ranking**; if payment buys placement or tilts methodology, disclosure does not cure it (§ 255.4 Example 3).  
- Any efficacy claims implied by placing the product next to a “you need this” band still need the same substantiation as if Seenmark said them outright.

### Gaps not verified from a primary source in this pass

- No FDA guidance example that squarely classifies a **consumer hairline photo score / early-mid-late band** as wellness vs device. Mapping is by analogy to General Wellness + MMA + § 520(o).  
- Whether a consumer scalp selfie is a “**medical image**” under § 520(o)(1)(E)’s CDS exclusion was not resolved beyond the statute’s text; the January 2026 CDS PDF examples were not successfully retrieved in full in this pass (landing page confirmed).  
- No FDA or FTC primary page found that regulates **clinic introduction / lead-handoff** as its own category.  
- No FDA/FTC primary source found that copies ABHRS’s “guaranteed results are inappropriate” rule for hair surgery; only general deception/substantiation and warranty-guarantee advertising guides.  
- State medical-practice, telehealth, and referral-fee / anti-kickback rules were **out of scope** (federal agency sources only).
